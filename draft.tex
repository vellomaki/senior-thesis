\section{Introduction}
We consider expletive \textit{there} contexs (hereafter TCs) such as (\ref{ex:tc}).\footnote{This essay is part of an ongoing senior thesis, the latest version available at \url{https://github.com/vellomaki/senior-thesis}. My immense thanks to Jess H.K. Law for advising and steering me in this process.} Since \citet{milsarkExistentialSentencesEnglish1974}, there has been substantial development in their exploration (see \cite{mcnallyExistential2011} for a some 120 citation bibliography).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There will be a duck roasted on Arbor Day.\\
		\null\hfill(\cite[ch.~1, ex.~12a]{milsarkExistentialSentencesEnglish1974})}\label{ex:tc}
\end{exe}

While the present essay does make some limited claims on TC with respect to novelty (and definites with respect to familiarity), the present essay rather concerns a rich syntactic product: relative clauses out of TCs (TRCs) as in (\ref{ex:trc}). To my knowledge first considered in \citet{carlsonAmountRelatives1977}, the phenomenon of interest is syntactically typified by a relative clause (RC) formation with a gap in the postcopular DP position of a TC:

\begin{exe}
	\ex {The people there were at that time only lived a few decades.\\
		\null\hfill(\cite[ex.~10a]{carlsonAmountRelatives1977})}\label{ex:trc}
\end{exe}

Even more narrowly, for reasons of space and time, we will only concern ourselves at present with TRCs' anti-indefiniteness effect:\footnote{In fact, much of the discussion is on specifically definite-indefinite contrasts with other determiners taken up towards the end of the essay in \S\ref{sec:determiners}.} simply put, definite \textit{the} (\ref{ex:trc}) and even universal \textit{every} (\ref{ex:det:universal}) but not indefinite \textit{a} (\ref{ex:det:indefinite}) determiners may cooccur with TRCs (and several other determiners in both camps, see \S\ref{sec:determiners}). Of course, such a restriction is simply not seen in ordinary restrictive relative clauses (\ref{ex:det:rrc}).

\begin{exe}
	\ex[] {Every lion there is eats meat.\hfill(\cite[ex.~10e]{carlsonAmountRelatives1977})}\label{ex:det:universal}
	\ex[*] {A man there was disagreed.\hfill(\cite[ex.~11b]{carlsonAmountRelatives1977})}\label{ex:det:indefinite}
	\ex[] {The/Every/A girl that climbed the tree proclaimed victory.}\label{ex:det:rrc}
\end{exe}

This anti-indefiniteness is particularly striking against the well-known (anti-)definiteness effect of TCs: indefinite \textit{a} (\ref{ex:tc}) but not/rarely definite \textit{the} (\ref{ex:de}) may occur in TCs.

\begin{exe}
	\ex[*] {There will be the duck roasted on Arbor Day.\footnote{Ignoring for the time so-called list readings.}\\
		\null\hfill(\cite[ch.~1, ex.~12b]{milsarkExistentialSentencesEnglish1974})}\label{ex:de}
\end{exe}

I claim that TRCs' anti-indefiniteness is simply a product of conflicting novelty requirements between the head determiner and the TC. Meanwhile, definites and universals do not have novelty conditions and thus do not conflict.

Finally, and important to the analysis, I consider (what I believe to be) novel evidence with regards to the extent of novelty and familiarity: First, (\ref{ex:det-projection}) is unacceptable when the cat in question would be completely novel. This will motivate particular definitions for familiarity and novelty of definites and TCs respectively. Specifically, I claim TCs exhibit index-novelty\footnote{I understand the literature to typically consider ``(discourse) referent'' to mean ``variable'' or ``index'' (such that the \(x\) of \(\exists x \phi\) is a ``referent''). I find this terminology strange and inviting confusion so avoid it in preference for ``variable'' and ``index.'' Likewise, I unambiguously say that that entity involved with a ``referent'' is the ``referred entity'' and similar periphrastics, or sometimes just ``entity.''} in the sense of \citet{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982} while definites' familiarity considers (merely) actual entities (coupled with a uniqueness check).

\begin{exe}
	\ex[\#] {The cat there is here has a hat.}\label{ex:det-projection}
\end{exe}

Second, (\ref{ex:definite-tc}) can be made acceptable without resorting to a so-call `list' existential typically used to license definites in TCs. This will require bringing attention to the role of the coda. I claim that TCs' index-novelty is constrained by the coda, the importance of which is highlighted by \citet{zucchiIngredientsDefinitenessDefiniteness1995}.

\begin{exe}
	\ex[\ok] {There is the cat there was here (now) in the office.}\label{ex:definite-tc}
\end{exe}


\section{Formalism}\label{sec:formalism}
I will present an analysis making use of dynamic semantics along the lines of \citet{groenendijkDynamicPredicateLogic1991}'s Dynamic Predicate Logic (DPL). Since I wish to establish how the formalism works in each case, I will preface with its introduction; waffling about its details can be found in \S\ref{sec:wafflings}. This said, much of the argument can be made without reference to formalism.

\subsection{Preliminaries}
\citet{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997} presents a dynamic semantics of TCs whereby discourse referent/variable introduction is closer to a presupposition. She analyzes the core assertion of TCs to be that a property \(be_{exist}\) of the postcopular DP (i.e. \textit{cat} in the running examples) is that it has a nonempty extension. Of course, this is the case if and only if there exists a referent \(y\) such that \(y\) is in said extension. Then, she argues, novelty is provided by \textit{there} in the form \(y \not\in D(c)\) where \(D(c)\) is the domain of entities in the context. Finally, if I am reading her analysis of the coda correctly, the domain of discourse \(D(c)\) has been sufficiently updated by the coda to align with how we have considered index-novelty to be constrained above.

While I do not at this time wish to adopt McNally's entire analysis, I will run with a few points:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item TCs introduce an index to the contextual domain of discourse \(D(c)\)
	\item TCs minimally entail \(\exists x \in D(c) \cup \{x\} [X(x) \land P(x)]\) where \(X\) is the interpretation of the coda (e.g. \textit{in the garden}) and \(P\) is the interpretation of the postcopular sortal (e.g. \textit{cat}).
	\item A TC updates the contextual domain of discourse \(D(c)\) so as to familiarize all \(X\) in \(D(c)\) by the time the process reaches novelty/\textit{there}.
	\item Novelty is understood as the condition \(x \not\in D(c)\) (equivalently \(x \not\in D(c') \cap X\) in the presence of a coda, where \(c'\) is the context before interpreting the TC).
\end{enumerate}

To my knowledge, the syntactic-semantic analysis of relative clauses is a bit of an open problem with regards to whether or not the head noun should reconstruct into the RC (see, e.g., \cite{bhattRaisingAnalysisRelative2002, cinqueSyntaxRelativeClauses2020} for discussion). For simplicity and concreteness, I will assume reconstruction, though the semantics should be workable for a variety of non-reconstructive analyses.


\subsection{Dynamic Predicate Logic}
I largely follow the dynamic predicate logic (DPL) of \citet{groenendijkDynamicPredicateLogic1991} (henceforth G\&S). In the goal of brevity and readability, I will identify a predicate \(R\) with its interpretation \(F(R)\); likewise \(F(t) = t\) for constants \(t\). I will further abbreviate \(\langle \llbracket t \rrbracket_g \rangle = \llbracket t \rrbracket_g\). Finally, I often skip over a number of obvious steps in derivations and omit a great many brackets and parentheses.

Let us define images and pre-images as usual: given a (possibly partial) function \(f : X \to Y\) (treating \(f\) as the underlying relation \(xfy\) on \(X\) and \(Y\) here), the \textit{image} of a set \(A \subseteq X\) under \(f\) is
\[f(A) \coloneqq \{y | \exists x \in X . xfy\},\]
meanwhile, the \textit{pre-image} of \(B \subseteq Y\) by \(f\) is
\[f^{-1}(B) \coloneqq \{x | \exists y \in B . xfy\}.\]
The \textit{range} of \(f\) is \(f(X)\) and the \textit{domain} of \(f\) is \(\dom(f) \coloneqq f^{-1}(Y)\)

Finally, I define the novelty \(\nu x\) and familiarity \(! x\) predicate-tests:

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\(\alpha\llbracket\nu x\rrbracket\beta \coloneqq (\forall y . y \in \dom \alpha \to \alpha(y) = \beta(y)) \land x \not\in \dom \alpha\) \\
		(\(\alpha\) does not assign a value to \(x\))}
	\ex {\(\alpha\llbracket! x\rrbracket\beta \coloneqq \alpha = \beta \land \beta(x) \in \alpha(\Sigma \setminus \{x\})\)\\
		(the entity \(\beta(x)\) is referred to by some other variable \(y \neq x\))}
\end{exe}


\section{TCs, Novelty, and Anti-Definiteness}\label{sec:tc-novelty}
To briefly recount, TCs are anti-definite in the sense that they exclude many definite and universal determiners while permiting many indefinite determiners. While accounts of these facts are about as wide-spread as theories of syntax-semantics, I will adopt some key points from \citet{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997}\footnote{This is a slightly updated version of her \citeyear{mcnallyInterpretationEnglishExistential1992} dissertation (\cite{mcnallyInterpretationEnglishExistential1992}).}, especially her consideration of TC-novelty and variable introduction.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There is a/some/*the/*that/*every cat at the door.}
	\ex {There are two/most/many/few/*all students in this class.}
\end{exe}

Our account of the definite-indefinite contrast above will be essentially that of \citet{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997} (among others) that TCs have a novelty condition which conflicts with the familiarity requirements of definites while being perfectly compatible with the novelty of indefinites (see \cite{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982} for discussion of novelty and familiarity). Specifically, again following McNally and Heim, I take up an index-based novelty filter/test to the effect of (\ref{rl:tc-novelty}).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\textit{TC-Novelty:} The index \(i\) of the postcopula DP position must not be part of the contextual domain of discourse \(D(c)\): \(i \not\in D(c)\).}\label{rl:tc-novelty}
\end{exe}

This directly corresponds to the conjunct \(i \not\in \dom \alpha\) of \(\nu i\) (the rest being effectively assignment-identity between input and output). Also, this rather directly relates to \citet[p.~100]{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982}'s Novelty Condition on indefinites that the index of an indefinite must be ``novel'' (not on any DP to its left) on the basis of data like (\ref{ex:indef-novelty}). Thus, TCs naturally permit indefinites.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {A cat_i is in the garden. John likes the cat_i, and Mary hates a cat_j.\\
		(*i=j)\hfill(Adapted from \cite[p.~101, ex.~13]{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982})}\label{ex:indef-novelty}
\end{exe}

I will actually follow the traditional interpretation of indefinites in DPL as \(\exists u\). Not to get into waffling, but we can consider this to mean indefinites don't formally care about getting a novel index, just that whatever may be at that index doesn't matter.\footnote{This is a non-issue if one adopts \(\nu x = \exists x\); see \S\ref{sec:wafflings}.} An example of the assignment update-process for a TC is given in Figure~\ref{fig:tc}.\footnote{I use fraktur characters \(\mathfrak{x}, \mathfrak{y}, \mathfrak{z}, \ldots \in D\) etc. to indicate entities. Further, see \S\ref{sec:assignments} for why \(g\) fails the rightmost update in Figure~\ref{fig:tc}; I will hereafter ignore such trivial \(g\) from demonstrations.}

% Note that the logic goes: (i) Definites are familiar/anti-novel. (ii) Indefinites are novel/anti-familiar. (iii) Definites and indefinites are identical except with respect to novelty/familiarity. (iv) Indefinites, but not definites, are allowed in construction \(R\). (v) Thus, \(R\) conflicts with the familiarity of definites. (vi) Therefore, \(R\) is anti-familiar. (vii) Novelty is at least as strong a condition as anti-familiarity. (viii) \textit{Ceteris paribus}, a stronger condition is more falsifiable. (ix) Metatheoretically, \textit{ceteris paribus}, more falsifiable theories are preferable. (x) Hence, the stronger of two conditions is prefered. (xi) This means (vii) entails that novelty is prefered over anti-familiarity. (xii) Then, we strengthen (vi) to the statement that \(R\) is novel. (xiii) That is, it is a valid rule of inference that, if construction \(R\) allows indefinites but not definites, then \(R\) is novel. (xiv) TCs allow indefinites but not definites, therefore TCs are novel by (xiii).

Meanwhile, again with Heim, definites have a familiarity condition. This minimally entails (\ref{rl:index-familiartiy}) (I show in \S\ref{sec:assignments} this is actually vacuous at the level of property interpretation and thus take up a different understanding of familiarity). Thus, TCs cannot (generally) take definite DPs. Ergo, the anti-definiteness effect observed.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\textit{Index-Familiarity:} The index \(i\) of a definite DP must be part of the contextual domain of discourse \(D(c)\): \(i \in D(c)\).}\label{rl:index-familiartiy}
\end{exe}

\subsection{Discourse Introduction and the Function of TCs}\label{sec:discourse-introduction}
I wish to not-so-briefly remark on the discourse introduction properties of TCs; though with exception to a final piece of evidence, its frankly derivative of much of the literature. The astute reader following along with their own judgements will observe that TCs are generally stunted and formal expressions. However, they need not be; in fact, they are at their most natural when introducing what I think are indices to the discourse. Under the context (\ref{context:catless-garden}), (\ref{ex:introducing-cat}) is completely unremarkable and properly introduce the cat (or at least its existence).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\textit{Context: We are in the garden and neither of us are aware of any cats being in the garden. I spot a cat.}}\label{context:catless-garden}
	\ex {There's a cat here in the garden.}\label{ex:introducing-cat}
\end{exe}

Of course, any claim of discourse introduction seems open to counterexamples via. negation as (\ref{ex:neg}). However, this merely suggests that any introduction shouldn't take quantificational scope (\cite[p.~107]{mcnallyInterpretationEnglishExistential1992}). Indeed, mere low-scope introduction implies we are negating that introduction.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There wasn't a sound.\hfill(\cite[ex.~192]{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997})}\label{ex:neg}
	\ex {\(\lnot \exists x . \text{sound}\,x\)}
\end{exe}

We should be somewhat careful as to whether TCs themselves (i.e. \textit{there}, \textit{be}, or a combination) induce any introduction or if it's simply the well-licensed indefinite. However, playing agnostic to any entity introduction, we see below that index-novelty is maintained in definites:

Rather strongly challenging the idea that the \textit{only} function of TCs is entity introduction, \citet{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997} illustrates a few examples employing deictics and definites plainly appearing in TCs:

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There were the same people at both conferences. \\
		\null\hfill(attributed to Prince~1992, ex.~5a)}
	\ex {Montanans are in a resentful mood... Then there is that resentment.\\
		\null\hfill(attr. \textit{Chicago Tribune}~9/4/92; Ward~\&~Birner~1995, exx.~8b,~14c)}
	\ex {A: What's on the office desk?\hfill(attr. Ward~\&~Birner~1995, ex. 31b)\\
		B: There's the telephone, but nothing else.}
	\ex {OK, lets finish up this guest list. \textit{There's you and me.} Who else is coming?\hfill(\cite[ex.~6]{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997}, italics original)}
	\ex {CT: It's just not something the Midland Symphony is going to be able to pull off.\\
		KC: That's true---there is that.\hfill(attr. overheard conversation)}
\end{exe}

These strongly indicate that TCs cannot categorically assert (even just hearer-) novelty of the referred entity/situation. However, I do not think they pose a problem for index-novelty: in each case the TC seems to ``take up'' the entity for discussion. Care must be taken with deictics and pronouns, themselves being inherently familiar indices; I will posit that we equate the index of the anaphor with that supplied by the TC \textit{but only through their referred entities}. Thus, the variable involved is new, even while the entity is old.

Finally, adapting a recent experience of mine: I have just arrived home and spot a car I don't recognize in the driveway. It wasn't there when I left, but my dad has been home all day so he should know. (A) I walk inside, where my dad presumably isn't thinking about cars parked outside, and ask (\ref{ex:car:there}); (\ref{ex:car:question}) is odd. Or, (B) before I get inside, my dad comes out to the trash, which happens to be right next to the driveway, suddenly I can only ask (\ref{ex:car:question}) and not (\ref{ex:car:there}).\footnote{Actually, I was \textit{planning} to ask my dad these questions. And, on second judgement, the unacceptability of (\ref{ex:car:question}) in (B) is not very strong; but the judgements were very strong in the moment.}

\begin{exe}
	\ex {Who's car is there in the driveway? (\ok A, *B)}\label{ex:car:there}
	\ex {Who's car is in the driveway? (??B, \ok A)}\label{ex:car:question}
\end{exe}

We see here the direct influence of changing salience, with no change to actual knowledge, completely shifting acceptability of TCs. This is cleanly analyzed assuming index-novelty of TCs: In (A), the car \(C\) is in common knowledge, but not the common ground; in particular, there is no discourse indexical assigned to \(C\). The TC of (\ref{ex:car:there}) properly provides an index which passes novelty and anaphoric reference to \(C\) is supported. However, the definiteness associated with the possessive in (\ref{ex:car:question}) seems to have difficulty in providing such a novel index. Do note that the familiarity of the definite possessive of (\ref{ex:car:there}) will be supported by the TC's novelty in the manner of TRCs (see the following section). Finally, the mere marginality of (\ref{ex:car:question}) in (B) suggests the indexation involved in \textit{wh}-questions might help support the definite possessive's familiarity, though the sheer contrast of especially (\ref{ex:car:there}) between (A) and (B) is the important data of the present argument.

Meanwhile, in (B), the salience of \(C\) being right next to both me and my dad would place it in the common ground with an indexical. Thus, (\ref{ex:car:there}) fails novelty and familiarity in (\ref{ex:car:question}) is well-supported.

\subsection{The Role of \textit{There}}
Let me round off by suggesting we wrap novelty into \textit{there} as suggested by McNally: Beyond copulative expletive \textit{there} sentences, verbal expletive \textit{there} sentences disallow definites and seem to have a novelty effect as they allow indefinites but not definties (\cite{milsarkExistentialSentencesEnglish1974}). Moreover, the first class \textit{arrive}, \textit{exists}, \textit{begin}, etc. are rather directly presenting the new DP into the discourse; but even stationary verbals \textit{stood}, \textit{hang}, etc. have a very ``stage presentation'' air about them. See too how the final remark of (\ref{ex:thereless-existential-be}) along with (\ref{ex:creature}) and (\ref{ex:let-it-be}) arguably contain that \textit{be} of compulative TCs but are without \textit{there} and seem to permit definites and pronouns. This is all expected if \textit{there} carries TC novelty.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\begin{xlist}
			\ex {There is a/*the man at the door.}
			\ex {There arrived a/*the man at the door.}
			\ex {There stood a/*the man at the door.}
			\ex {There walked a/*the man to the door.}
		\end{xlist}}
	\ex {A: Apparently they found a new ruined-city, do you know it? \\
		B: I know of it, but I don't know it. \\
		A: What do you know of it? \\
		B: Mostly that it is.}\label{ex:thereless-existential-be}
	\ex {I don't know man, the creature just is.}\label{ex:creature}
	\ex {Let it be.}\label{ex:let-it-be}
\end{exe}


\subsection{Recap}
We saw that TCs along with indefinites carry an index-novelty condition (\(i \not\in D(c)\)) along the lines of \citet{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982, mcnallyInterpretationEnglishExistential1992}. As by \citet{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997}, any novelty of TCs should \textit{not} be understood in terms of referred-entity novelty. Meanwhile, definites carry some kind of familiarity condition. If familiarity at least entails non-index-novelty (\(i \in D(c)\)), then we have a natural account for why indefinites but not definites are permitted in TCs.


\section{TRCs, Familiarity, and Anti-Indefiniteness}
We saw above that index-novelty in TCs induced anti-definiteness. \textit{Ceteris paribus}, we expect a relative clause formed out of a TC (i.e. those TRCs) to also exhibit anti-definiteness. However, this is exactly opposite the facts. In fact, we will see that TRCs \textit{cannot} introduce novel discourse entities. On the inverse, if TRCs carry familiarity, they should be outlawed by contexts of indefiniteness, however again, we see (\ref{ex:tc-trc}) may accept TRCs when they themselves are embedded in other TCs (see below for stronger context).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {The/*A cat that there was here had a hat.}\label{ex:trc:def}
	\ex {There's the cat there was in the garden now in the office.}\label{ex:tc-trc}
\end{exe}

Do we simply abandon familiarity and novelty? I claim no, and rather point out that index-novelty creates a grey-zone as to entity-novelty/famliarity. Let us explore more carefully what is going on in by following the development of discourse and knowledge in context in the following sections.

\subsection{Prelude}
One may note that Heim shifts definites' commonly attested uniqueness (\(\forall y . P(y) \to y=x\)) to familiarity on point that uniqueness is not actually necessary for definites: in the context of being surrounded by our cats, so long as there is a uniquely ``salient'', ``most likely'', or ``relevant'' (or something along that sense) cat, definites may be produced:

\begin{exe}
	\ex {The cat has a hat.}\footnote{It is in fact a leaf.}
\end{exe}

\subsection{Introductory TRCs}\label{sec:as-mentioned}
We pointed out above that TCs introduce indices. Let us return to the garden. Should I (but not you) spot an orange cat with a hat \(C\) in the otherwise uninhabited garden I may completely inoccuous remark (\ref{ex:unremarkable}). However, (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) would be a presently strange utterance. What is interesting for the present purposes is that the TRC (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) is unacceptable as an out-of-the-blue statement.
% TODO: and as a follow-up to (\ref{ex:unremarkable}). 
It seems the familiarity of definites is not observed thereby leading to unacceptability. Note too that if \(C\) becomes salient and enters the common ground, say by jumping on your sweater, (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) is still unacceptable, now by TC index-novelty.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There's a cat here in the garden.}\label{ex:unremarkable}
	\ex {The cat there is in the garden has a hat.}\label{ex:blue:intro}
\end{exe}

\subsection{Recollective TRCs}\label{sec:recall}
Let us fix my uttering (\ref{ex:unremarkable}) in the past and skip forward to a context where \(C\) is nonsalient and thus without index. (\ref{ex:blue:def}) would be an odd utterance presumably by familiarity as there is no index referring to \(C\). However, (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) is perfectly natural.

\begin{exe}
	\ex[?] {The cat in the garden had a hat.}\label{ex:blue:def}
	\ex[] {The cat there was in the garden had a hat.}\label{ex:blue:trc}
\end{exe}

Let me highlight this unremarkability: readers following along with theirown judgements may find TRCs to be generally strange---informants repeatedly report they are ``unnatural'', a plain RC without \textit{there} or even just a prepositional phrase (PP) would be better, or simply ``I wouldn't say that.'' Yet here is a context wherein the TRC (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) is substantially more natural than the analogous PP.

Now, the question is, why is (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) better than (\ref{ex:blue:def})? That is, how do TRCs support definites' familiarity? This is where the considerations of dynamic semantics and the development of intra-sentential discourse come into play: I claim that before the utterance of (\ref{ex:blue:trc}), \(C\) is truly unfamiliar---the contextually established index for \(C\) is not readily available---however \(C\) is in the common ground, supported by \textit{some} index by virtue of my earlier remark (\ref{ex:unremarkable}). This non-familiarity passes the novelty conditions of the embedded TC and thus \(C\) is introduced to the discourse. Having just been (re-)introduced, \(C\) is sufficiently familiar to pass definiteness.

It should be noted that (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) cannot refer to any absolutely novel (i.e. unknown by you) entity\footnote{Well, (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) could be uttered out-of-the-blue but it would require accomadation. Various responses are felicitous: \textit{There was a man in the garden?} or \textit{Which man?}} as seen with (\ref{ex:blue:intro}). This is very odd. For some reason, (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) can support \textit{recalling} an entity, but it can't \textit{introduce} that entity. Whatever novelty/introductory force TRCs have, the definiteness presupposition still projects. If we followed \citet{heimSemanticsDefiniteIndefinite1982}'s account of the index \(i\) either being in the domain \(D(c)\) or not as the novelty-familiarity paradigm, there are three routes: (i) handle TC-novelty and definite-familiarity simultaneously, in which case we have the immediate contradiction which yields TCs' anti-definiteness; (ii) handle novelty before familiarity, in which case we support (\ref{ex:blue:def}) but overgenerate (\ref{ex:blue:intro}); or, (iii) handle familiarity before novelty, which blocks (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) but overly restricts (\ref{ex:blue:trc}). Trying to abadon novelty/familiarity conditions, first note that this drops the explanation for TCs' anti-definiteness, but it also loses any explanations of any of the TRC facts.

I claim that we don't need to go searching in an entirely different realm for accounts of the empirical phenomena; rather, we need a richer notion of familiarity. I will maintain novelty as index-based as well-established by Heim (and others), as we have extended indefinites' novelty to TCs, and I will maintain the above claim that this novelty supports familiarity. This leaves us with a case of overgenerating (\ref{ex:blue:intro}).

My solution is to shift familiarity from indices to entities. Recall from \citet{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997} that TCs independently can pick up known entities. If definites must (also) be familiar \textit{entities} and not (just) familiar indices, then they should never be able to introduce totally novel entities (without accomadation). Thus is (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) ruled out.


\subsection{The Extent of Novelty}\label{sec:novelty}
Finally, let us return to TRCs embedded in other TCs: Again, let me have remarked (\ref{ex:unremarkable}). Should I go on to spot that orange garden cat \(C\) in the office, I might inform you:

\begin{resexe}
	\item[(\ref{ex:tc-trc})] There's the cat there was in the garden now in the office.
\end{resexe}

Now, just above, we argued that the definite is licensed by novelty from the embedded TC making \(C\) sufficiently familiar. Yet we argued in \S\ref{sec:tc-novelty} that definiteness-familiarity directly conflicts with TC-novelty thereby yielding TCs' anti-definiteness facts. The puzzle is clear: How is \(C\) made familiar/non-novel enough to license the definite yet novel/non-familiar enough to license the TC? I claim that it is precisely as claimed: \(C\) is non-familiar and non-novel, existing in a grey-zone of familiarity-novelty. Or rather, \(C\) is perfectly familiar, thereby licensing the definite; but completely novel \textit{in so far as the discourse restriction of the coda `in the office' is concerned}.

The discourse-restrictive import of the TC-coda is rather convincingly argued in \citet{zucchiIngredientsDefinitenessDefiniteness1995}. And indeed, TC-novelty is mediated by the coda: \(i\) and \(j\) in (\ref{ex:coda-reference}) may corefer (if not formally coindex) despite the particular referent being non-novel.\footnote{For pragmatic reasons, the contexts should not be uttered during judgement: Pre-theoretically, these yield oddness judgements; by common sense, it doesn't make sense to use an extistential to refer to an indefinite just introduced in the discourse when a definite or pronoun would perfectly suffice. Within the theory, TCs require novel indices which might refer to a familiar entity (see discussion in \S\ref{sec:discourse-introduction} with \cite{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997}). Pragmatically, Gricean Quantity prevents arbitrary index introduction, though of course Relevance might take hold in the other direction. Semantically, we might appeal to some form of \textit{Maximize Presupposition}.} Meanwhile, it is odd for them to corefer in (\ref{ex:coda-conflict}). Assuming the coda mediates novelty---i.e. the domain of the anti-familiarity of TCs is determined by the coda---these facts and the original TC-embedded TRC are directly predicted: the referents they introduce are not known to be within the domain of the coda and hence are sufficiently novel to be licensed in the TC.

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\textit{Context: A man_i was in the garden.}\\
		There is (now) a man_j in the office.}\label{ex:coda-reference}
	\ex {\textit{Context: A man_i walked into the office.}\\
		There is (now) a man_j in the office.}\label{ex:coda-conflict}
\end{exe}

\subsection{Viz. List Existentials}\label{sec:list}
\citet{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997} in her Chapter~5 argues that TCs' novelty (or rather referent introduction for her) can be ``turned off'' as it were just in case it is ``presupposed to be true in the context in which it is uttered'' (her ex.~327). This serves to explain so-called list-existentials (\ref{ex:list}).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {(\cite[ex.~322]{mcnallySemanticsEnglishExistential1997})
		\begin{xlist}
			\ex {Who is there available to fix the computer?}
			\ex {Well, there's Alice.}
		\end{xlist}}\label{ex:list}
\end{exe}

I do not think such an analysis will suffice for explaining TRCs and even TC-embedded TRCs. First of all, the TC-embedded TRC (\ref{ex:tc-trc}) does not apparently read as a list-existential. It is an assertion of \(C\)'s new location, not a sublist of, say, (\ref{ex:sublist}).

\begin{resexe}
	\item[\textit{Context.}] I remarked \textit{there is a cat in the garden} earlier, it is now in the office.
	\item[(\ref{ex:tc-trc})] There's the cat there was in the garden now in the office.
\end{resexe}

\begin{exe}
	\ex {Well, there's the cat there was in the garden, Mittens, and Tigger in the office.}\label{ex:sublist}
\end{exe}

Moreover, I'm rather doubtful of McNally's assertion that we can ever just ``turn off'' novelty. Bringing back our discussion of \citet{abbottDefinitenessExistentials1997} from \S\ref{sec:discourse-introduction}, this would have to a range of distinct contexts, the only clearly consistent feature of which is that they are non-novel entities. Except then why is it still so odd to use a definite in a TC even when referring to non-novel entities?

\begin{exe}
	\ex {A cat_i walked into the room. There is ??the cat_i/*it_i in the room.}
\end{exe}

\subsection{Interim Summary}
We have seen a number of facts about TCs and TRCs:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	%\item TCs introduce a discourse referent (Lemma~\ref{lem:referent}).
	%\item That discourse referent is novel, possibly as the meaning of expletive \textit{there} (Lemma~\ref{lem:novel}).
	\item Indefinites have a novelty condition.
	\item Definites have a familiarity condition, rather than the traditional strict uniqueness.
	\item TC-novelty and definite-familiarity are closely related so as to yield TCs' definiteness facts.
	\item Despite their TC embedded content, TRCs cannot introduce novel entities out-of-the-blue \S\ref{sec:as-mentioned} (\ref{ex:blue:intro}).
	\item TRCs cannot immediately refer to a salient/indexed entity, indicating TC-novelty is still at issue \S\ref{sec:as-mentioned} (\ref{ex:blue:intro}).
	\item TRCs can recall non-familiar entities \S\ref{sec:recall} (\ref{ex:blue:trc}).\label{sum:novel-recall}
	\item TC-novelty is mediated by the domain of the coda \S\ref{sec:novelty} (\ref{ex:coda-reference}-\ref{ex:coda-conflict}; \cite{zucchiIngredientsDefinitenessDefiniteness1995}).
	\item This domain mediation allows TRCs to embed in TCs with disjoint codas \S\ref{sec:novelty} (\ref{ex:tc-trc}).
	\item TRCs (embedded or not) should not appeal to an analysis whereby novelty and/or referent introduction are ignored.
\end{enumerate}

Our account noted in \S\ref{sec:recall} of the felicity of definites with TRCs is exactly that the TC introduces a novel index for a familiar entity. It is clear then that the novelty effect of indefinites conflicts with the novelty from the embedded TC thereby yielding TRCs' anti-indefiniteness.

In the next section I will sketch a formalism to the facts within a dynamic semantics. We conclude in \S\ref{sec:determiners} by accounting for the other determiner selections then some final remarks in \S\ref{sec:mysteries} on further problems.

\section{Wafflings on the Formalism}\label{sec:wafflings}
\subsection{Assignment Functions}\label{sec:assignments}
Note that assignment functions are well-defined in G\&L whereas I allow them to be merely partial, i.e. having being undefined on some \(v \in \Sigma\). This is because index-novelty of \(v\) is defined such \(v\) is not in the domain \(\dom(g)\) of the input \(g\) in systems like Heim's and McNally's. G\&L handle index-novelty in a destructive manner via. random resets \(g[x]h\). Precise formal-theory distinction between partial and well-defined \(g\) comes down to a bunch of waffling as I see it.\footnote{We can also throw into the ring here assignment to empty individuals ``\(v \mapsto \varepsilon\)'' as an alternative to ``\(g(v)\) undefined.'' But then we have to juggle \(\varepsilon\) around in the rest of the logic. Note further we have to distinguish \(\varepsilon\) being a formal entity \(\varepsilon \in D\) vs. merely a possible assignment \(g : \Sigma \to D \cup \{\varepsilon\}\) (\(g\) well-defined).}

Metatheoretically, following Heimian index-familiarity accounts of definiteness, \(i \in \dom(g)\) is vacuous if \(\dom(g) = \Sigma\) (of course, I argue this is vacuous anyway given treatment of relating variables to properties via assignment functions). Meanwhile, index-novelty as \(g[x]h\) is destructive on the value of \(x\),\footnote{I can neither prove nor disprove such; I am simply not comfortable enough in the metatheoretic philosophy behind DPL. \(\nu\) might be equatable with \(\exists\) in DPL, but one must be careful that Heim's indefinites-facts hold.} which seems to just shift the issue to index assignment; this may be desirable.


\subsection{Vacuity of Index-Familiarity}
I have repeatedly made mention to the fact that index-familiarity of the form \(i \in \dom g\) is vacuous given variables and predicates being mediated by \(g\). I prove this in the following theorem, but let me first note the definition of a partial function: a \textit{partial function} \(f : X \to Y\) is a relation between \(X\) and \(Y\) such that \(xfy \land xfz \to y = z\). \(f\) is said to \textit{undefined} on \(x \in X\) if and only if \(\lnot \exists y . xfy\). Finally, equality and function application: \(\exists y . xfy \to f(x) = y\) (by definition, this \(y\) is unique).

\begin{theorem}
	Given a partial function \(f : X \to Y\) undefined on \(x \in X\) and a set \(A \subseteq Y\), \(f(x) \not\in A\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	As given, \(f\) is undefined on \(x\). That is, \(\lnot \exists y . fxy\). By definition of equality and function application then, \(\lnot \exists y . f(x) = y\).

	By way of contradiction, assume \(f(x) \in A\). Therefore,
	\[f(x) \in A \implies \exists y . f(x) = y \land y \in A\]
	by existential introduction. In particular,
	\[\exists y . f(x) = y \land y \in A \implies \exists y . f(x) = y.\]
	However, it is given that \(\lnot \exists y . f(x) = y\). Thus, we have a contradiction. Hence, \(f(x) \not\in A\).
\end{proof}

One can easily extend this result as appropriate to \(A \subseteq Y^n\) by induction, however I only use and thus only state the base case. An immediate corollary considers explicit assertions of well-formedness:

\begin{corollary}
	Given a partial function \(f : X \to Y\) and set \(A \subseteq Y\),
	\[x \in \dom f \land f(x) \in A \equiv f(x) \in A.\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	Observe that \(x \not\in \dom f\) if and only if \(f\) is undefined on \(x\). Thus, by the theorem, if \(x \not\in \dom f\), then \(f(x) \not\in A\). By the contrapositive, if \(f(x) \in A\), then \(x \in \dom f\). Since the first conjunct is materially implicated by the second, it is vacuous. That is,
	\(x \in \dom f \land f(x) \in A \equiv f(x) \in A\).
\end{proof}

We may then immediately put this corollary in the context of DPL:

\begin{corollary}
	Given a partial assignment function \(g : \Sigma \to D\) and a 1-place predicate \(R\),
	\[i \in \dom g \land g(x) \in R \equiv g(x) \in R.\]
\end{corollary}

That is, \(i \in \dom g\) is vacuous given predicate interpretation with variables \(\llbracket R x \rrbracket\) is mediated by an assignment function. So while we could define familiarity \(!x\) to conjoin with \(i \in \dom g\), that is immediately vacuous as soon as that assignment \(g(i)\) is actually called upon. Even a minimal utterance \textit{Her_i.} isn't merely asserting \(i \in \dom g\). This brings us to our definition of entity-based familiarity.

\subsection{Entity-Based Familiarity}
Our definition of familiarity \(g\llbracket ! x\rrbracket h \coloneqq g = h \land h(x) \in g(\Sigma \setminus \{x\})\) is odd coming from the Heimian index-familiarity tradition. I reason that this is the appropriate understanding of familiarity in DPL. (i) As seen in the previous section, Heimian \(i \in \dom g\) is largely vacuous, so we do need a new account of familiarity, or else ellide it from the formal language. I also point out that (ii) entity-familiarity (though not quite this form) is implied by Heimian index-familiarity: if a phrase has a familiar index, then it must be referred to a familiar entity---i.e. that entity referred by the familiar index. Finally, this is almost exactly the position of anaphoric definites such as outlined by \citet{lawWeNeedTwo2025}: a definite noun phrase is assigned itsown index \(u\) and is interpreted as a composition of index instantiation \(\exists u\), anaphoric reference \(u = v\) to another index \(v\), and a uniqueness check \(1_u\) on \(u\) as in (\ref{ex:law-def-interpretation}).

\begin{exe}
	\ex {\(\llbracket \text{\(\text{the}_v^u\) student}\rrbracket = \lambda P. 1_u (\exists u \cdot u = v \cdot S u) \cdot P u\)}\label{ex:law-def-interpretation}
\end{exe}

My entity-familiarity \(! u\) is largely equivalent to \(\exists u \cdot u = v\) just without explicit instantiation nor an anaphoric index---for any \(v\), \(u = v\) by virtue of incidental coreference, effectively shifting the role of anaphoric index assignment to the calculation of the space of assignment functions \(\mathcal{G}\). This said, I will maintain the present approach as it more cleanly handles the interaction of novelty and familiarity in TRCs. Were that definites \textit{the^u_v} inherently introduced an independent variable related by anaphora, we would be stuck explaining how the definite \textit{the^u_{v,w}} of TRCs is simultaneously anaphoric to the RC content (held by \(w\)) and whatever other index \(v\) supports the entity-familiarity.


\subsection{Interpreting TC Novelty}
It should be clear from our above discussion that the appropriate interpretation, with respect to an assignment function \(g \in \mathcal{G}\), of TCs with novelty to be equivalent to
\begin{exe}
	\ex {\(\exists x . g(x) \not\in D(c) \cap X \land X(g(x)) \land P(g(x)),\)}\label{sem:tc-novelty}
\end{exe}
where \(D(c)\) is the contextual domain. This is equivalent to
\begin{align*}
	 & g \models \nu x \cdot X x \cdot P x                                                                                                        \\
	 & \equiv \exists g' . g \llbracket\nu x \cdot X x \cdot P x\rrbracket g'                                                                     \\
	 & \equiv \exists g'\alpha\beta . g \llbracket\nu x\rrbracket\alpha\llbracket X x\rrbracket\beta\llbracket P x\rrbracket g'                   \\
	 & \equiv \exists g'\alpha\beta . g[x]\alpha \land \alpha x \not\in g(\Sigma) \land \alpha = \beta \land X\beta x \land \beta = g' \land Pg'x \\
	 & \equiv \exists g' . g[x]g' \land g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \land Xg'x \land Pg'x                                                               \\
	 & \equiv \exists g' . (g[x]g' \land g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \land Xg'x \land Pg'x) \lor \bot                                                   \\
	\begin{split}
		 & \equiv \exists g' . [(g[x]g' \land g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \land Xg'x \land Pg'x)                             \\
		 & \qquad\lor (g[x]g' \land \underbrace{g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \land g'x \not\in X}_\bot \land Xg'x \land Pg'x)
	\end{split}                                \\
	 & \equiv \exists g' . g[x]g' \land (g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \lor  g'x \not\in X) \land Xg'x \land Pg'x                                         \\
	 & \equiv \exists g' . g\llbracket\exists x\rrbracket g' \land g'x \not\in g(\Sigma) \cap X \land Xg'x \land Pg'x                             \\
\end{align*}

It should be clear that this is equivalent to () once converted back to classical logic, where \(D(c) = g(\Sigma)\).

\subsection{Familiarity}\label{sec:familiarity}
One should recall from \S\ref{sec:recall} that the TC in (\ref{ex:blue:intro}) cannot introduce a completely novel referent, but (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) can recall that referent \(B\). How is this supposed to work? I previously claimed that \(B\) is unfamiliar but in the common ground in (\ref{ex:blue:trc}) thereby licensing the reference. Let me make that sense more precise.

\begin{itemize}
	\item[(\ref{ex:blue:intro})] The man there is in the garden has a hat.
	\item[(\ref{ex:blue:trc})] The man there was in the garden had a hat.
\end{itemize}

I maintain that novelty is computed with respect to the relevant domain of individuals
\[\alpha\llbracket \nu x \rrbracket\beta \coloneqq \alpha[x]\beta \land \beta(x) \not\in D^\alpha.\]
However, I will updated the familiarity predicate to reference the entire history \(\mathcal{H}\) of interpretation functions: Minimally, \(\mathcal{H}\) should include those minimal \(g \in \mathcal{G}\) which are a/the result of interpreting a discourse. A discourse \(\phi\) is minimally an utterance, e.g. (\ref{ex:unremarkable}) \textit{There's a man here in the garden.} For minimality, we may define a partial order on \(\mathcal{G}\) by \(D\)-containment \(\subseteq\): \(\alpha < \beta\) iff \(D^\alpha \subseteq D^\beta\). Therefore, we say that \(g \in \mathcal{G}\) is \textit{minimal} on \(A \subseteq \mathcal{G}\) iff \(g \in \inf A\).

Thus, taking \(\phi\) to be the discourse of (\ref{ex:unremarkable}) (or its LF anyhow), those minimal \(g \in \mathcal{G}\) such that \(g \models \nu x \cdot \text{here-in-the-garden}\,x \cdot \text{man}\,x\) are members of \(g \in \mathcal{H}\).\footnote{This waffling is a necessary condition on \(\mathcal{H}\), presumably many more assignment functions are in \(\mathcal{H}\) from sub-discourse levels.} By minimality, the image \(g(\Sigma)\) of such a \(g\) will be just that individual introduced by \(\phi\) (and any in preceeding context).

Now, we define familiarity with respect to \(\mathcal{H}\) as so:
\[\alpha\llbracket!x\rrbracket\beta \coloneqq \exists \gamma \in \mathcal{H} . \beta(x) \in D^\gamma \cup D^\alpha.\]

\subsection{Non-Issue: Amounts}
As syntactically qualified, some TRCs admit amount readings wherein (\ref{ex:amount}) most saliently reads (\ref{ex:amount:head}):

\begin{exe}
	\ex {There isn't the water in the sink that there is in the bathtub.\\
		\null\hfill(Lisa Selkirk, p.c. to Irene Heim; so cited in \cite{vonfintelAmountRelativesMeaning1999})}\label{ex:amount}
	\ex {There isn't the amount of water in the sink that there is in the bathtub.}\label{ex:amount:head}
\end{exe}

I will pointedly ignore these cases\footnote{I will also briefly remark that \citet{carlsonAmountRelatives1977}'s two diagnostics other than anti-indefiniteness are a relativizer restriction (\textit{that}, null; never \textit{wh}-forms) and anti-stacking facts (TRCs may not stack with intersective import). I ignore them as beyond the present scope and as I indpendently have tentative reason to believe something more complicated is going on.} as (i) I believe individually-read TRCs should be schismed from amount relative analyses (see, ongoing senior thesis) and (ii) out of an abundance of caution for what exactly might be going on in amount readings (see \cite{mendiaAmountRelativesRedux2017} for a thorough examination of RCs with amount readings in English and Spanish). In a sense, this essay and the greater thesis present a position against necessarily lumping TRCs with amount RCs in order to get the anti-indefiniteness, as has been historically appealed to (\cite{carlsonAmountRelatives1977, heimWhereDoesDefiniteness1987, grosuStrangeRelativesThird1998, grosuAmountRelatives2017}).
